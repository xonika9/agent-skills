# The method

A restatement of the approach in Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (Russian edition: «Спроси маму»). Points marked *(not from the book)* come from wider interview practice.

## Three rules

1. Talk about their life, not your idea. Mention the idea late or not at all.
2. Ask about specific past events, not about what they usually do, think, or would do.
3. Talk less and listen. Talking less means no pitching, not silence: steering questions and follow-ups are still talking.

## Questions that work

Useful shapes, adapted to the domain:

- The last time it happened: walk through it from start to finish.
- How they deal with it now, and what they like and dislike about that.
- What they have already tried, what it cost, and why they stopped.
- What the problem costs them in money, hours, or nerves.
- Why they bother at all, and what happens if they do nothing.
- Where the money for this comes from and who else takes part in the decision (business purchases).
- What else you should have asked; who else you should talk to.

Follow-ups do most of the work: why, what happened next, can you show me, when was the last time.

**Confirm before zooming in.** Asking for the details of a problem before learning whether it matters to the person makes a minor annoyance look important. First ask what their biggest concerns in the area are; dig into the user's problem only if it appears there.

**Questions you dread.** A plan without a question that could sink the idea is a plan for collecting reassurance. Name the answer the user least wants to hear, such as "I have never paid anything to fix this", and put the question that could produce it near the start, while a bad answer can still redirect the work.

**Desk research first.** Anything a search can answer, such as market size or the existing competitors, is not worth a person's time.

## Bad data and how to handle it

| Kind | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Compliments | "What a great idea", "I love it" | Praise is about the relationship, not the problem; leave it out of the evidence and steer back to how they cope today. |
| Generic claims | "I usually", "I always", "I never" | Ask for the most recent concrete instance and walk through it. |
| Future promises and hypotheticals | "I would definitely buy", "I might", "we plan to" | Ask what they have done about it so far; a promise is worth what has already been done to back it. |
| Ideas and feature requests | "You should add…" | Note the request, then find the need behind it: what they want it for and how they manage without it. Build for the need, not the wording of the request. |

A vague positive that survives being anchored to a concrete instance becomes a fact; one that does not was noise. If a claim has no date, amount, or event attached, keep digging.

*(Not from the book.)* Answers tend to come in layers: first a polite surface ("it was fine"), then exaggeration in either direction, and only after several follow-ups the actual events and their causes. Do not stop at the first two layers.

The interviewer produces much of the bad data. Fishing for approval ("wouldn't that be handy?") and creeping into a pitch mid-conversation ("and it also does…") buy compliments with the conversation's time. Name both in a debrief when they happen.

## Signals worth digging into

- **Strong emotion**: anger at the current tool, embarrassment, visible excitement. Always ask what is behind it.
- **What they already do about it**: a homemade spreadsheet, a script, a paid service, a person hired for the job. The more effort someone has already put in, the more the problem hurts. Someone who has done nothing about a problem is telling you it is tolerable, even while agreeing it exists.
- *(Not from the book.)* **Details left hanging**: something mentioned in passing and then dropped. Come back to it later; people often skip on out of modesty and have plenty to say.
- *(Not from the book.)* **Unprompted mentions**: a problem raised before you hinted at it counts for far more than agreement with one you named.

## Commitment and advancement

Once a solution is discussed, opinions stop counting. A meeting succeeds when it ends with:

- **Commitment**: the person gives up something of value.
  - Time: another meeting with a clear purpose, real use of a trial, a working session.
  - Reputation: an introduction to a colleague, a manager, or the person who decides; putting their name behind you publicly.
  - Money: paying in advance, a deposit, a signed intent to buy, a paid pilot.
- **Advancement**: a concrete next step that moves toward a purchase or adoption.

Weigh what people say by what it costs them to say it. *(Not from the book.)* Before the meeting, pick three asks: an ambitious one, a reasonable one, and the smallest step that still counts as progress. Failing to get even the smallest one tells you something about this person or segment.

Warm goodbyes with no next step are refusals in disguise: "keep me posted", "let me know when it launches", "I'd definitely use it". Someone is a real prospect only after you offered them a concrete step they could have turned down. A clear no beats a maybe: a maybe keeps a contact alive without ever moving it forward. Asking is not pushing; back off rather than burn the relationship.

## Segments

When the segment is too wide, answers pull in different directions and nobody knows where to find these people. Narrow it by asking who in the group wants this most, why, what they already do about it, and where they can be found. Stop narrowing when two people from the segment would give similar answers and you can name a place to meet them. If a series of conversations keeps contradicting itself, cut the segment finer instead of averaging the answers or adding conversations.

## Finding people and framing the conversation

- Casual beats formal. A five-minute chat at an event can teach as much as a scheduled hour, because nobody performs. Ask for advice, not for an interview, and drop the word "startup" early on.
- Sources: the existing network, people who once offered help, communities and events where the segment gathers, sign-ups from a landing page, and an introduction requested at the end of every conversation.
- When a meeting must be requested, the message carries five parts: the goal in a few words, an honest statement of where you are and that nothing is being sold, what you are stuck on, why this particular person can help, and a concrete small request.
- Length follows the goal: a few minutes confirm whether the problem exists and matters; ten to fifteen minutes cover workflow and past attempts; an hour suits a deep session with an industry expert.

## Notes and review

- Write during the conversation or right after it; memory keeps what confirms the idea.
- Keep their exact words and numbers. "Lost two evenings a month matching payments by hand" can be checked against the next conversation; "has trouble with payments" cannot.
- Mark notes with the symbols in [templates](templates.md#note-symbols).
- Keep facts apart from interpretations. Review notes with the team soon, update the risks, and choose the next three big questions.

## When to stop talking

Stop and act when answers repeat, when you can predict the next conversation, when the segment is clear, and when people are committing. Many conversations without clarity mean the segment is too broad. More conversations past that point are often a way to postpone building.

## Signs of going through the motions

- You talk more than the other person.
- They compliment you or the idea.
- After describing the idea you do not know what happens next.
- There are no notes, or nobody reviewed them.
- An unexpected answer changed nothing in the plan.
- No question you asked frightened you.
- You cannot say which big question the conversation was meant to answer.
- Meetings "went fine" but nobody gave up anything of value.

## Where the method bends

- An expert's opinion about their own field carries weight; the rule targets opinions about your idea.
- A product team talking to existing users already knows the product is wanted; the questioning rules still apply, while commitment and segment narrowing matter less.
- Formal enterprise sales, regulated industries, and some cultures expect structured meetings; keep the questioning rules and accept the format.
- For something truly new, people cannot picture it; ask about the workarounds and costs around the problem rather than about the solution.
